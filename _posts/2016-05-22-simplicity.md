title: Reflections on Simplicity
date: 2016-05-22 13:30:09
category: Reflections
---

Sometimes the best solution is the simplest one.

Back in high school, I took an introductory engineering course called Principles of Engineering. We would be assigned projects every few weeks and were graded on how well our solution solved the problem.

One of the earlier projects we had was designing a marble sorter. We were given lego-like blocks, sensors, motors, and a programmable controller to build our sorter with. The sorter had to sort 5 each of four different types of marbles: metal, wood, clear plastic, and opaque acrylic.

Our group worked on a sorter that would use a photodetector to narrow down the possibilities and then finally use a gradually widening gap to separate by size. During the building process, we realized that the quality of the parts we had weren't very good. It was hard to build accurate tracks using the plastic blocks we had, and because of that, the marble would sometimes be shifted the wrong way, and the sensor would give incorrect readings. We tried some normalization techniques, but it made the project more complicated without helping too much. In the end, we miscategorized a little over a quarter of the marbles on our final demo.

I watched another group's demo that I thought was absolutely brilliant. Unlike all the other groups, they didn't use any electronics and had built a sorter that relied on the marble's speed, size, and bounce instead. It only relied on gravity and the simple properties of the marbles that worked reliably.

Later on in the year, we were tasked to a ping pong ball launcher. It had to be adjustable to hit a target up to 10 feet away. Our group quickly drafted and built a design that launched the ball using two motors with spinning rubber wheels by squeezing the ball out from in between.

However, like the previous project, there were unexpected problems that we didn't account for. Sometimes one motor would be slower than the other and would launch the ball sideways. We were unable to do anything fix it because the motors could only just be turned on/off. Sometimes the wheel would hit a small dent in the ping pong ball and it wouldn't be launched as far. It really messed with our calculations for how much the launcher should be tilted to hit a target at a particular distance, so the design had to be scrapped.

I thought back to the marble sorter project and suggested a simpler launcher that used gravity to launch the ball - a weight would swing to "kick" the ball, and we could change the height that weight swung from to control how far the ball will be launched. We were able to build our new design easily and no additional problems appeared.

I really liked the outcome of this project because it was so simple, yet it worked perfectly. If we had higher quality parts to work with, solving our problems would have been easier, but working with additional constraints was a good experience.
